We asked whether language models that simulate members of Congress represent the members whose economic and social votes point in different directions. In three open models, they largely do not. Each model's map follows the roll-call record and recovers most aligned members, but it places only {{recall_cross}} of cross-pressured member-Congress rows in their voting quadrant. When the prompt states a cross-pressured profile, the models follow it less faithfully than an aligned one, and in Qwen and OLMo a choice between crossed packages is more uncertain than a choice between party-line packages even without a legislator persona. Llama is the exception in the last analysis: with personas, its crossed choices are less uncertain, and without a persona its interval includes zero.

**Who the simulated Congress misses.** The members at issue are not an abstract category. The counts in this subsection come from a descriptive follow-up computed after the main analyses were sealed (Appendix B.5). In the roll-call record from the 93rd Congress on, {{elst_dem}} of the {{elst_all}} rows that are economically left and socially traditional belong to Democrats, and {{ersp_rep}} of the {{ersp_all}} rows that are economically right and socially progressive belong to Republicans. Cross-pressure in this period mostly means Democrats whose abortion votes leaned toward restriction and Republicans whose abortion votes leaned toward access. The senators among the legislators we selected by their votes illustrate both groups (Appendix B, Table B2, lists all 60 legislators). The Democrats are Thomas Eagleton of Missouri, Walter Huddleston of Kentucky, Jennings Randolph of West Virginia, John Pastore of Rhode Island, Howell Heflin of Alabama, and John Melcher of Montana. The Republicans are Robert Packwood of Oregon, John Chafee of Rhode Island, and Hiram Fong of Hawaii. Only Randolph is placed in his own quadrant by all three models. Of all 30 cross-pressured legislators selected by their votes, none of the models places {{cross_none}} correctly ({{cross_none3}} are misplaced by all three and {{cross_none_missing}} have no OLMo position) and only {{cross_all3}} is placed correctly by every model, whereas {{aligned_all3}} of the 30 aligned legislators are placed correctly by every model.

Where the models put these legislators is informative as well. Packwood, whose votes place him among economically right and socially progressive members, appears in all three maps among economically right and socially traditional members. The same happens to most misplaced cross-pressured Republicans ({{rep_to_erst}}), who land in the quadrant that is the usual position of their party. For these members, the map matches the economic record but not the abortion record. Misplaced Democrats are less uniform. Llama and OLMo move most of them into the aligned Democratic quadrant ({{dem_to_elsp_lo}} in Llama and {{dem_to_elsp_oo}} in OLMo), so that a Democrat like Eagleton appears there as an economically left and socially progressive senator. Qwen places Eagleton correctly but sends other Democrats in both directions, and Huddleston appears in all three maps among economically right and socially traditional members.

Many of the legislators the models miss served early in the sample. Of the 30 cross-pressured legislators selected by their votes, {{cross_1970s}} have their first observation in our sample, which begins in 1973, in the 1970s, when the parties' positions on abortion were still forming (Adams, 1997; Layman & Carsey, 2002). The share of cross-pressured rows falls from {{share_70s}} in 1973 to 1979 to {{share_10s}} from 2011 to 2021 (through the 117th Congress, the last with an abortion scale). Over the same decades the maps recover aligned rows increasingly well ({{ra_70s_10s}}), while the recall of cross-pressured rows stays low ({{rc_range}}) and is lowest from 2011 to 2021 in every model (Table B6). The maps therefore fit the sorted Congress of recent decades better than the Congress of the 1970s, mainly because they recover aligned members better and those members are more common.

**What the results say about the four accounts.** The results narrow the accounts in Section 2 without selecting one. Missing information is not enough, because stated cross-pressured profiles are still followed less faithfully than aligned ones. Combination receives partial support: crossed packages are more uncertain in two models, and choices about stated cross-pressured profiles are more uncertain in two models, but the uncertainty of generated text rises only slightly, below the preregistered minimum in every model. Dominance fits both the pattern for misplaced Republicans and the economic side of most decided crossed choices. Default fits the first of these as well. As anticipated in Section 2, the map cannot separate the two, because for these members the economic position and the party's package point to the same quadrant. On the map, the two cross-pressured quadrants differ only slightly in recall; for stated profiles, the two differ in some ways of stating the profile and not in others, so the results give direction no consistent support. Unfamiliarity remains possible, since crossed packages are harder for two models without any persona.

**Implications for simulated political actors.** The failure that agreement with the aggregate ordering of members can hide falls on named legislators, such as Packwood, Chafee, Eagleton, and Huddleston, and it is visible when each member's own votes serve as the benchmark and cross-pressured members are examined as a group. This pattern resembles findings on simulated survey respondents. Synthetic data can match averages while missing relationships (Bisbee et al., 2024), and simulated voters who do not conform to their party are predicted poorly (von der Heyde et al., 2026). It differs from the finding of Rennard and Xypolopoulos (2026) that, once sampling noise in the human benchmark is accounted for, counter-stereotypical combinations of identities carry no extra simulation error. Their benchmark is the answer distribution of demographic groups, whereas ours is the record of individual legislators, and with that benchmark the atypical members are the ones the models miss.

**Implications for the study of Congress.** A first dimension correctly classifies most votes (Poole & Rosenthal, 1991), but a single score cannot place a member whose economic and abortion votes diverge on both dimensions at once, and low-dimensional summaries can underemphasize issues that divide the parties internally (Aldrich et al., 2014) and the complexity of individual bill votes (Roberts et al., 2016). The simulated legislators fail with these members even though they produce two coordinates. Their two coordinates move together, so their maps place few members in the cross-pressured quadrants (Section 4). Consider a researcher who simulates how the Senate of the 1970s would vote on an abortion measure. Senators who were cross-pressured in that decade, such as Huddleston (in our sample from 1973 to 1979), Packwood, and Eagleton, are among those the maps most often misplace, averaged over their years in the sample: Huddleston and Packwood in all three models, and Eagleton in two.

**Conclusion.** In the three open models we study, simulated members of Congress reproduce the aligned structure of the chamber and miss its cross-pressured members, and the models follow stated cross-pressured profiles less faithfully than aligned ones. The cross-pressured members they miss are mostly Democrats whose abortion votes leaned toward restriction and Republicans whose votes leaned toward access, a group that was largest before the parties sorted on the issue.
